A Controlled Study of Variational Quantum Circuits as
Q-Value Approximators in Deep Q-Learning
1

Abstract. This paper reports a controlled study of a hybrid Deep Q-Learning
agent where the classical Q-network is replaced by a Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC). Focusing on CartPole-v1, we compare a DQN-VQC agent against
a parameter-matched MLP-DQN baseline (22–23 trainable parameters), and
evaluate Double DQN and Dueling DQN variants for both model types. At 100
episodes, both agents collapse below random under a tight epsilon schedule. Extended training to 500 episodes recovers learning in MLP (greedy eval 69.45 in
one seed) while VQC remains below random, though with high seed variance. A
batched QNode optimization reduces the VQC simulation overhead from 44× to
5× relative to MLP. In a separate supervised distillation experiment, the same
VQC perfectly imitates a fuzzy heuristic controller (500.00 over 3 seeds) with
descriptive membership features, for both L1 (22 params) and L3 (46 params)
circuit depths. The contrast highlights a gap between representational capacity
and what DQN reward signals extract at minimal training budgets.
Keywords Quantum Reinforcement Learning, Variational Quantum Circuits,
Deep Q-Learning, CartPole-v1, Dueling DQN, Double DQN

1. Introduction
Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) combines reinforcement learning with function approximation and has become a standard approach for learning control policies from interaction data. In Deep Q-Learning (DQN), a neural network is used to approximate
action-value functions, making it possible to act in environments with continuous or highdimensional observations. In parallel, Variational Quantum Circuits (VQCs), also known
as parametrized quantum circuits, have been studied as trainable models for near-term
quantum machine learning.
This work evaluates a conservative question: if the Q-network of a DQN agent is
replaced by a small VQC, and the classical baseline is constrained to a similar number of
trainable parameters, how do the two agents compare in sample efficiency, stability, and
training time on a simple control task?
The goal is not to claim quantum advantage. The experiments are performed with
a classical simulator and a small number of qubits. Instead, the contribution is a controlled
implementation and empirical comparison of a DQN-VQC agent against a parametermatched MLP-DQN baseline, including a transparent report of negative or inconclusive
results.
This framing is important because small simulated VQCs are not expected to outperform classical networks in this setting. The relevant question is whether the hybrid
agent can be integrated into a standard DQN workflow and what practical costs or instability appear under a controlled parameter budget.

2. Background and Related Work
DQN [Mnih et al. 2015] estimates the optimal action-value function with a bootstrapped
Bellman target, experience replay, and a separate target network. These mechanisms reduce correlation between samples and stabilize temporal-difference updates. Extensions
such as Double DQN [van Hasselt et al. 2016] and Dueling DQN [Wang et al. 2016] address overestimation bias and improve representation efficiency.
VQCs provide a different family of differentiable function approximators. A classical input is encoded into a quantum circuit, trainable gates transform the quantum state,
and measurements are mapped back to classical outputs. In a hybrid setting, a VQC can be
combined with classical layers and optimized with gradient-based methods through automatic differentiation. Data re-uploading [Pérez-Salinas et al. 2020] repeats the encoding
step at each variational layer, increasing the circuit expressivity.
Prior work on quantum reinforcement learning has investigated VQCs in lowdimensional Gym-style environments [Chen et al. 2020, Jerbi et al. 2021, Lockwood e Si 2021]
and has reported that design choices such as data encoding, circuit depth, data re-uploading,
and readout strategy can strongly affect learning. This paper follows that line of work,
but keeps the scope intentionally narrow: one low-dimensional environment, one DQN
pipeline, and a parameter-matched classical baseline. Notably, we also implement and
evaluate Double DQN and Dueling DQN variants with the quantum Q-function, and we
report batched QNode execution as a practical optimization for simulated VQCs in RL
workloads.

3. Methodology
Both agents use the same DQN training loop: replay buffer, epsilon-greedy exploration,
a target network, Adam optimization, and Huber loss for the Bellman error. Observations
are normalized before being passed to the Q-network. For CartPole-v1, the four state
variables are scaled by fixed practical bounds and clipped to the interval [−π, π].
The MLP baseline is a single-hidden-layer network. For the main L1 configuration, it uses 3 hidden units, resulting in 23 trainable parameters. This intentionally small
network was chosen to match the parameter budget of the VQC.
The VQC agent uses 4 qubits, angle encoding with RY gates, one variational
layer, and a ring of CNOT gates. Each variational layer applies a trainable three-parameter
rotation to every qubit. The readout consists of Pauli-Z expectation values for all qubits
followed by a classical linear layer that maps the measured features to Q-values. The main
VQC configuration has 22 trainable parameters. The circuit evaluation was optimized
via batched QNode execution (PennyLane parameter broadcasting), reducing the per-step
overhead from per-sample QNode calls to a single batched call during each DQN update.
We additionally implemented two DQN variants: Double DQN and Dueling DQN.
Double DQN decouples action selection from value estimation by using the policy network for action choice and the target network for evaluation, which reduces overestimation bias. Dueling DQN splits the Q-network output into a state value V (s) and a per1 P

In addition to the DQN experiments, we include a separate fuzzy-teacher distillation experiment. A fixed CartPole fuzzy heuristic labels sampled states with a reference
action, and the small MLP or VQC is trained as a classifier to imitate this teacher. We
evaluate two input representations: the raw normalized state and a four-dimensional fuzzy
membership representation describing whether the pole is falling left/right and whether
the cart is escaping left/right. The membership representation does not include a recommended action. This experiment does not replace the RL comparison and is not reported
as DQN performance. Its purpose is to test whether the same low-parameter models can
represent a useful fuzzy control policy when explicit domain knowledge is supplied by a
teacher.

4. Experimental Setup
The experiments use CartPole-v1 from Gymnasium. The observation space has 4 continuous variables and the action space has 2 discrete actions. The main reported configuration
uses 100 training episodes and 3 random seeds (0, 1, 2).
Both agents use discount factor γ = 0.99, learning rate 10−3 , replay buffer size
50,000, batch size 16, target update interval 100 environment steps, and epsilon-greedy
exploration from 1.0 to 0.05 over 800 environment steps. After training, each learned
policy is evaluated greedily (ϵ = 0) for 20 episodes. The same random seeds are used for
the classical and VQC agents.
The classical MLP was executed with PyTorch on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX
5090. The VQC was simulated with PennyLane ‘default.qubit’ on CPU. The software
stack used PyTorch 2.12.0+cu130, Gymnasium 1.3.0, and PennyLane 0.45.0 in the local
environment.
The reported metrics are reward per episode, final mean reward over the last 20
training episodes, greedy evaluation reward, trainable parameter count, and wall-clock
training time. Random and heuristic policies are included as reference baselines. A separate larger MLP run is used only as a sanity check that the DQN implementation can learn
when the parameter budget is relaxed.

confirms that both agents collapsed to a near-deterministic single-action policy, which

We identify the exploration schedule as a key driver of this collapse. At 100
episodes with these model sizes, the total number of environment steps is approximately
1200, and the epsilon decay reaches its minimum after 800 steps. This means the agent
spends the final third of training with ϵ = 0.05, effectively locked into its exploratoryphase policy. We confirm this by extending training to 500 episodes with a proportionally
scaled epsilon schedule (decay over 4000 steps). One MLP seed out of three reached a
greedy evaluation of 69.45, while the other two remained at approximately 9.3. VQC
runs over 500 episodes showed modest improvement (up to 13.95) but remained below
random in all cases.
These results suggest that under the 22–23 parameter budget, final policy quality
is dominated by the exploration schedule, training length, and random seed rather than
by the classical-vs-quantum choice. Short training runs with tight epsilon schedules are
particularly fragile for small models.

Computational cost and batched QNode optimization
The main practical difference between MLP and VQC is computational cost. The parametermatched MLP runs took 1.72 seconds on average, while the simulated VQC runs with
the original per-sample QNode evaluation took 75.97 seconds on average. After implementing PennyLane parameter broadcasting for batched circuit evaluation (replacing
the per-sample QNode loop in quantum features with a single batched call using qml.RY(inputs[:, wire], wires=wire)), VQC runs dropped to approximately 9.55 seconds on average. The per-environment-step cost was approximately
0.0014 seconds for the MLP and 0.008 seconds for the batched VQC. While still approximately 5× slower than MLP, this represents a significant improvement over the original
44× gap and demonstrates the importance of batched quantum circuit evaluation in RL
settings, where the Q-network forward pass is called with minibatch sizes of 16 during
each DQN update.
Fuzzy-teacher distillation
Table 4 reports fuzzy-teacher distillation scenarios. In this setting, the small models are
not trained from reward alone; instead, they imitate a fixed fuzzy heuristic controller via
supervised cross-entropy loss. With raw normalized inputs, the L1 VQC improves over
random (96.53 ± 38.13) but remains far from the teacher. With the descriptive fuzzy
membership representation, the L1 VQC reaches 500.00 ± 0.00 over 3 seeds with 22
trainable parameters. We additionally tested VQC L3 (three variational layers, 46 parameters) with fuzzy membership features and confirmed the same 500.00 ± 0.00 across 3
seeds. This shows that increased quantum depth does not degrade representation quality
under supervised distillation. The MLP with fuzzy membership reaches a lower 440.72
± 102.68, with high variance.
These distillation results should be interpreted as a fuzzy-teacher representation
benefit rather than a quantum advantage or DQN learning from reward. The fuzzy membership features make the task near-linearly separable, and the same 22-parameter VQC
that fails to learn via DQN can perfectly represent the fuzzy controller when given structured supervision. The key insight is that the representational capacity of these small
models exceeds what the DQN reward signal can effectively exploit at this training budget.

6. Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, the VQC was simulated classically; therefore,
wall-clock time does not represent execution on quantum hardware. Second, the experiment uses only CartPole-v1 and a small number of seeds due to the submission timeline.

Third, the parameter budget is extremely small, which makes the comparison controlled
but also limits the representational capacity of both agents. In the main experiment, both
learned agents perform below a random policy, so the results characterize a failure regime
rather than a successful policy-learning regime. Fourth, only a shallow VQC is used in
the main experiment. Deeper circuits may be more expressive, but they also increase
simulation cost and may suffer from optimization issues such as vanishing gradients.
The results should therefore be interpreted as a small controlled empirical study,
not as evidence for or against quantum advantage in reinforcement learning.
The fuzzy-teacher distillation experiment has a different status from the DQN
experiments. It demonstrates that small models, including a VQC, can imitate a useful
fuzzy controller, but it uses supervised labels from a hand-designed teacher. Therefore, it
cannot be used to claim that the VQC learned the control policy from reward alone. The
fuzzy membership representation used in the main positive result avoids direct actionrecommendation features, but it is still a domain-informed representation and should be
reported as such.

7. Conclusion
This paper implemented and evaluated a DQN-VQC agent against a parameter-matched
MLP-DQN baseline on CartPole-v1. Under a 22–23 parameter budget and 100 training
episodes, both agents collapsed below a random-policy baseline in greedy evaluation — a
result traced to the interaction between small model capacity, a tight epsilon decay schedule, and short training length. Extending training to 500 episodes with a proportionally
scaled epsilon schedule recovered learning in one MLP seed (greedy evaluation 69.45)
while VQC remained below random, though both showed high variance across seeds.
Double DQN and Dueling DQN variants, implemented for both MLP and VQC,
did not improve outcomes in the 100-episode regime, confirming that the training budget
— not the value-estimation technique — dominates results at this scale. We also introduced batched QNode evaluation via PennyLane parameter broadcasting, which reduced
the VQC simulation overhead from approximately 44× to 5× compared to MLP, a practical optimization for VQC-based RL experiments in classical simulation.
As an additional fuzzy-teacher experiment, the same low-parameter VQC reached
the teacher’s maximal CartPole performance (500.00 over 3 seeds) after supervised distillation with descriptive fuzzy membership features. This result held for both L1 (22
parameters) and L3 (46 parameters) circuit depths, showing that increased quantum depth
does not degrade representational quality under structured supervision. The contrast be-

tween this supervised result and the DQN failure highlights a gap between representational capacity (the model can encode a good policy) and what the DQN reward signal
can extract at this training budget.
Future work should increase the number of seeds to 5–10, tune the DQN hyperparameters per budget, extend to additional Gymnasium environments, and investigate
whether hybrid training strategies — combining reward-based DQN with fuzzy-guided
exploration or curriculum learning — can bridge the gap between representation and effective learning for small VQC models.

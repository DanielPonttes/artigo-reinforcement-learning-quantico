## Verdict: **Weak, but defensible if reframed and one core experiment is fixed.**

The honesty is genuinely a strength — no quantum-advantage overclaim, baselines present, negative results reported, simulation cost flagged. For an undergraduate track, a clean "we built a hybrid DQN-VQC pipeline and report a controlled cost/viability study with negative results" is a legitimate paper.

**But there is one problem that, left unaddressed, justifies rejection on its own:** your headline result ("both learned agents collapse *below random*") is almost certainly a **training artifact, not a property of small VQCs/MLPs** — and your own config files prove it. This is the thing a sharp reviewer will catch, and it undermines the central narrative. Fix the framing around it and the paper becomes defensible. Leave it as-is and a reviewer concludes "the authors mis-trained their agents and then drew a conclusion from the failure."

Let me justify that bluntly.

## The core problem: the "below random" result is a tuning artifact

Your main config `cartpole_mlp_l1_eval100.yaml` has:
- `hidden_sizes: [3]` (23 params)
- `epsilon_decay_steps: 800`

CartPole episodes when the policy is bad are ~10–20 steps, so 100 episodes ≈ 1000–2000 environment steps total. With ε decaying to 0.05 by step **800**, the agent stops exploring roughly a third of the way through training and then spends the rest of training (and *all* of greedy eval) locked into a 3-unit network that has collapsed onto a **single action**. A constant-action greedy policy on CartPole scores ~9 steps; random scores ~20. That is exactly your 9.4-vs-20.6 gap, and the tiny std (±0.20) confirms a near-deterministic degenerate policy. This is policy collapse, not "small models can't represent the task."

Your own files contradict the headline:
- `cartpole_emergency` (`hidden_sizes: [6]`, `epsilon_decay_steps: 10000`) → final reward **22.0 ± 2.4** at only 50 episodes, *above* random.
- `fuzzy_optimized_combined_summary` → DQN-MLP greedy eval **225 ± 245**, DQN-VQC **232 ± 217**.

So whether the agent "collapses below random" or reaches ~225 is governed by the ε schedule and a couple of hidden units — i.e., hyperparameters — not by the quantum/classical choice or the 22-param budget per se. **A linear Q-function over 4 normalized CartPole inputs (≈10 params) is already enough to balance the pole.** 22 parameters is not inherently too few; the training recipe is the bottleneck.

**Why this matters for the verdict:** the paper currently draws a qualitative conclusion ("characterizes a failure regime") from what is really a poorly-tuned baseline. A reviewer who reads your configs will not trust the comparison. You must either (a) re-tune both agents to their honest best at the fixed budget and report *that* comparison, or (b) explicitly reframe the collapse as a **sensitivity finding** ("at this budget, outcome is dominated by the exploration schedule, not the function approximator") and back it with a small sweep.

## Suggested paper framing

Drop "failure regime" as the organizing idea. Reframe as **two clean, modest contributions**:

1. **A controlled cost study.** "We integrate a VQC as a DQN Q-approximator and quantify the *simulation* cost of doing so, including a batched-QNode optimization that cuts the per-step overhead ~3.4×, narrowing the VQC↔MLP wall-clock gap from ~44× to ~5×." This is real, reproducible, and genuinely useful engineering content — lean into it. The batching result is arguably your most solid quantitative contribution; give it a figure.

2. **A sensitivity finding at tiny budgets.** "At a ~22-parameter budget on CartPole, final policy quality is dominated by the exploration schedule and training length rather than by the classical-vs-quantum choice; VQC and MLP are statistically indistinguishable in return while VQC costs ~5× more to simulate." That is honest, supported, and avoids the trap of claiming the models "can't learn."

Then position fuzzy distillation as a clearly-labeled **side experiment**: "structured priors let compact models (both VQC and MLP) represent useful controllers" — explicitly *representation*, *supervised*, *not RL, not quantum-specific*.

The through-line: **"We are not claiming advantage; we are measuring what it costs and what actually drives outcomes when you bolt a VQC into a standard DQN."** That sentence is defensible and the whole paper should serve it.

## Presentation recommendations

- **Lead the abstract with the cost/sensitivity contribution, not "below random."** "Below random" sounds like a bug to a skimming reviewer; "outcome is schedule-dominated; VQC costs 5× more for equal return" sounds like a finding.
- **Add a learning-curve figure with episodes on the x-axis to 500** (item 2) overlaid with random/heuristic bands. This single plot does more than the three near-identical small tables.
- **Collapse your ~6 result tables into 2.** One main comparison table (MLP/VQC/random/heuristic: params, return, greedy eval, time, seeds) and one fuzzy-distillation table. The current proliferation invites the cross-table inconsistencies noted above.
- **Give the batched-QNode result its own small bar/table** (before vs after, per-step time) — it's your most concrete win.
- **In the VQC circuit figure caption,** state qubits/layers/encoding/readout and the parameter count so it's self-contained.
- **Report ddof and seed count in every table caption.** With 3 seeds, also consider showing per-seed points, not just mean±std.
- **State the heuristic baseline's role once, clearly** — it's a ceiling reference, not a learned competitor; right now it visually dominates tables and can confuse.

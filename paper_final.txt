A Controlled Study of Variational Quantum Circuits as
Q-Value Approximators in Deep Q-Learning
Anonymous Authors
1

Abstract. This paper reports a controlled study of a hybrid Deep Q-Learning
agent where the classical Q-network is replaced by a Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC). Focusing on CartPole-v1, we compare a DQN-VQC agent against
a parameter-matched MLP-DQN baseline (22–23 trainable parameters), and
evaluate Double DQN and Dueling DQN variants for both model types. At 100
episodes, both agents collapse below random under a tight epsilon schedule. Extended training to 500 episodes recovers learning in MLP (greedy eval 69.45 in
one seed) while VQC remains below random, though with high seed variance. A
batched QNode optimization reduces the VQC simulation overhead from 44× to
5× relative to MLP. In a separate supervised distillation experiment, the same
VQC perfectly imitates a fuzzy heuristic controller (500.00 over 3 seeds) with
descriptive membership features, for both L1 (22 params) and L3 (46 params)
circuit depths. The contrast highlights a gap between representational capacity
and what DQN reward signals extract at minimal training budgets.
Keywords Quantum Reinforcement Learning, Variational Quantum Circuits,
Deep Q-Learning, CartPole-v1, Dueling DQN, Double DQN

1. Introduction
Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) combines reinforcement learning [Sutton e Barto 2018]
with function approximation and has become a standard approach for learning control
policies from interaction data. In Deep Q-Learning (DQN) [Mnih et al. 2015], a neural network is used to approximate action-value functions, making it possible to act in
environments with continuous or high-dimensional observations. The DQN framework
has been extended with techniques such as Double Q-Learning [van Hasselt et al. 2016]
to reduce overestimation bias and Dueling architectures [Wang et al. 2016] to improve
representation efficiency by separately modeling state values and action advantages.
In parallel, Variational Quantum Circuits (VQCs), also known as parametrized
quantum circuits, have been studied as trainable models for near-term quantum machine
learning. VQCs encode classical data into a quantum state, apply sequences of trainable
unitary transformations, and extract classical outputs via measurement. Their differentiability through parameter-shift rules or backpropagation [Pérez-Salinas et al. 2020] makes
them compatible with gradient-based optimization in standard deep learning frameworks.
Recent work has investigated replacing classical neural networks with VQCs in reinforcement learning [Chen et al. 2020, Jerbi et al. 2021, Skolik et al. 2022]. These studies suggest that VQCs can serve as Q-value approximators in DQN-style algorithms,
though the conditions under which they offer practical benefits remain unclear, particularly under tight parameter budgets.
This work evaluates a conservative question: if the Q-network of a DQN agent is
replaced by a small VQC, and the classical baseline is constrained to a similar number of

trainable parameters, how do the two agents compare in sample efficiency, stability, and
training time on a simple control task?
The specific research questions are:
1. Under a matched parameter budget of approximately 22–23 trainable parameters,
do DQN-MLP and DQN-VQC agents show statistically distinguishable performance on CartPole-v1?
2. Do Double DQN and Dueling DQN variants alter the relative ranking between
classical and quantum Q-functions at this budget?
3. What is the practical simulation cost of using a VQC as a Q-function approximator, and can batched circuit evaluation reduce this overhead?
4. To what extent does the representational capacity of small VQC models exceed
what the DQN reward signal can effectively exploit, as measured by supervised
distillation from a known-good teacher?
The goal is not to claim quantum advantage. The experiments are performed with
a classical simulator and a small number of qubits. Instead, the contribution is a controlled
implementation and empirical comparison of a DQN-VQC agent against a parametermatched MLP-DQN baseline, including Double and Dueling variants with VQC backends, a batched QNode optimization that substantially reduces simulation overhead, a
transparent report of negative or inconclusive results, a supervised distillation experiment
isolating representational capacity from reward-based learning, and a fully reproducible
codebase with YAML-configurable experiments and comprehensive tests.
This framing is important because small simulated VQCs are not expected to outperform classical networks in this setting. The relevant question is whether the hybrid
agent can be integrated into a standard DQN workflow and what practical costs or instabilities appear under a controlled parameter budget. The paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 reviews background and related work. Section 3 describes the methodology,
including DQN training, VQC architecture, and DQN variants. Section 4 presents the
experimental setup. Section 5 reports and discusses the results. Section 6 lists limitations,
and Section 7 concludes.

Variational Quantum Circuits (VQCs) provide a different family of differentiable
function approximators. A classical input is encoded into a quantum state via feature maps
(e.g., angle encoding with RY rotations on each qubit), trainable gates transform the state
through layers of parametrized unitaries, and measurements (typically Pauli-Z expectation
values) produce classical output features. Data re-uploading [Pérez-Salinas et al. 2020]
repeats the encoding step at each variational layer, increasing expressivity. VQCs face the
barren plateau problem [McClean et al. 2018], where gradients vanish exponentially with
circuit depth in randomly initialized circuits.
Table 1 summarizes prior work on VQC-based reinforcement learning. The present
work differs by including Double DQN and Dueling DQN variants with a VQC backend, reporting batched QNode execution as a practical optimization, and including a supervised distillation experiment to isolate representational capacity from reward-based
learning.
The VQC-based DQN architecture in this paper follows the angle-encoding plus
ring-CNOT design common to prior work, with the addition of batched QNode evaluation
for simulation efficiency and systematic evaluation of DQN variants. The batched QNode
optimization replaces per-sample circuit calls with a single batched call using PennyLane
parameter broadcasting, a practical contribution relevant to any VQC-in-the-loop RL simulation.

3. Methodology
Both agents use the same DQN training loop, summarized below: replay buffer, epsilongreedy exploration, a target network, Adam optimization, and Huber loss (smooth L1) for
the Bellman error. Observations are normalized before being passed to the Q-network.
For CartPole-v1, the four state variables are scaled by fixed practical bounds and clipped
to the interval [−π, π].
The DQN loop proceeds as follows. After each environment step, the transition
(s, a, r, s′ , d) is stored in a fixed-capacity replay buffer D. Once |D| ≥ learning starts, a
minibatch of B transitions is sampled uniformly from D. The Bellman target is yj = rj +
γ(1−dj ) maxa′ Qθ− (s′j , a′ ) for standard DQN, or yj = rj +γ(1−dj )Qθ− (s′j , arg maxa′ Qθ (s′j , a′ ))
for Double DQN, where θ− denotes the target network parameters, updated every tarP
get update steps. The loss is L = B1 j Huber(Qθ (sj , aj ), yj ), minimized via Adam

with gradient clipping. Exploration follows linear ϵ-decay from 1.0 to 0.05.
The MLP baseline is a single-hidden-layer network. For the main L1 configuration, it uses 3 hidden units, resulting in 23 trainable parameters. This intentionally small
network was chosen to match the parameter budget of the VQC. For the Dueling variant,
the network trunk feeds two separate heads: a value head V (s) ∈ R and an advantage

VQC Architecture
The VQC agent uses 4 qubits, angle encoding with RY gates, one or three variational layers, and a ring of CNOT gates. Each variational layer applies a trainable three-parameter
rotation R(α, β, γ) = RZ(γ)RY (β)RZ(α) to every qubit. The readout extracts Pauli-Z
expectation values ⟨Zi ⟩ ∈ [−1, 1] for all qubits, yielding a 4-dimensional classical feature
vector that is then mapped to Q-values by a linear layer. The main VQC configuration has
4 × 3 = 12 trainable gate parameters plus 4 × 2 + 2 = 10 readout parameters, totaling 22
trainable parameters. The L3 variant uses three layers (3 × 4 × 3 = 36 gate parameters)
for 46 total parameters.
Batched QNode Optimization
The original implementation evaluated the QNode once per sample in each training batch.
For a batch of size B, this required B separate calls to the PennyLane QNode, each building a separate automatic differentiation computation graph. We replaced this with a single
batched call using PennyLane parameter broadcasting: the input tensor of shape [B, 4] is
zero-padded to [B, 4] → [B, 4] (no padding needed since nqubits = 4) and passed to the
QNode as [B, nqubits ]. The encoding gate RY (inputs[:, i]) naturally broadcasts across
the batch dimension, producing expectation values of shape [B, nqubits ] in a single forward pass. This optimization reduced the per-update VQC overhead from O(B) QNode
calls to O(1).

towards higher-reward trajectories without affecting the learned policy evaluation.

4. Experimental Setup
The experiments use CartPole-v1 from Gymnasium. The observation space has 4 continuous variables (cart position, cart velocity, pole angle, pole angular velocity) and the
action space has 2 discrete actions (push left, push right). Episodes terminate when the
pole angle exceeds ±12◦ , the cart position exceeds ±2.4, or 500 steps are reached. The
reward is +1 per step, making the maximum episode return 500.
Both agents use the hyperparameters listed in Table 2.
We evaluate eight main configurations, summarized in Table 3.
All configurations use 3 seeds (0, 1, 2). The 100-episode configurations use ϵ decay over 800 environment steps, which with approximately 10–20 steps per early episode
means epsilon reaches 0.05 within the first 40–80 episodes. The extended 500-episode
runs use proportionally scaled decay over 4000 steps.
The classical MLP was executed with PyTorch on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090
(GPU). The VQC was simulated with PennyLane default.qubit backend on CPU.
The timing comparison therefore conflates GPU-vs-CPU and PyTorch-vs-simulator overheads; reported VQC time multipliers should be interpreted as upper bounds on the simulation cost. The software stack used PyTorch 2.12.0+cu130, Gymnasium 1.3.0, and
PennyLane 0.45.0.

Table 5 summarizes the main results over 3 seeds. At 100 episodes with approximately 22–23 trainable parameters, both learned agents scored below the random-policy
baseline (20.65 ± 2.01) in greedy evaluation. DQN-MLP reached 9.42 ± 0.20 and DQNVQC reached 9.45 ± 0.18. Inspection of the learning curves and per-episode rewards
confirms that both agents collapsed to a near-deterministic single-action policy, which
explains the sub-random performance: a constant-action policy on CartPole-v1 survives
approximately 9 steps, while a random policy alternates actions and survives approximately 20 steps. The heuristic controller reached the maximum score of 500.00 and is
included only as a ceiling reference, not as a learned agent.
We identify the exploration schedule as a key driver of this collapse. At 100
episodes with these model sizes, the total number of environment steps is approximately
1200, and the epsilon decay reaches its minimum after 800 steps. This means the agent
spends the final third of training with ϵ = 0.05, effectively locked into its exploratoryphase policy. We confirm this by extending training to 500 episodes with a proportionally
scaled epsilon schedule (decay over 4000 steps). One MLP seed out of three reached a
greedy evaluation of 69.45, while the other two remained at approximately 9.3. VQC
runs over 500 episodes showed modest improvement (up to 13.95) but remained below
random in all cases.
These results suggest that under the 22–23 parameter budget, final policy quality
is dominated by the exploration schedule, training length, and random seed rather than
by the classical-vs-quantum choice. Short training runs with tight epsilon schedules are
particularly fragile for small models.
Table 6 reports the Double DQN and Dueling DQN variants under the same L1
budget and 100-episode training. Neither Double DQN nor Dueling improved greedy
evaluation performance in this short training regime. All variants scored approximately
9.25–9.65, similar to the standard DQN baseline. The Dueling architecture increased
the parameter count from 23 (MLP) / 22 (VQC) to 27 each due to the split value and
advantage heads. The identical performance between standard, Dueling, and DoubleDueling VQC configurations (9.42 ± 0.20 in all three) reflects the collapse to a singleaction deterministic policy, which is unaffected by the value-decomposition or decoupledestimation improvements.

These results should be interpreted carefully. The fuzzy membership features provide a domain-informed representation where the classification problem becomes nearlinearly separable. Under this representation, the VQC bridges the gap between raw
features and the teacher policy. Under raw features, the VQC fails even in supervised
distillation, indicating that its raw-input representational capacity is insufficient at this
circuit depth and qubit count. In contrast, the classical MLP with the same raw features
and extended training reaches the teacher, suggesting a raw-feature learning advantage
for the classical model.
The key insight is that the DQN failure on raw features is partly a reward-signal
extraction problem, but for the VQC specifically, it also reflects a raw-feature representational limitation confirmed by the distillation control. The distillation results therefore
measure representation quality, not DQN learning from reward, and should not be interpreted as quantum advantage.
Per-seed analysis
The extended 500-episode runs reveal substantial seed dependence. MLP seed 0 shows
a distinct learning trajectory: reward increases qualitatively after episode 300, with the
final 10 training episodes averaging 162.30 and the greedy evaluation reaching 69.45
over 17,646 environment steps. Seeds 1 and 2 remain at approximately 9.3 (6,160–6,265
steps), indicating policy collapse. The VQC run showed uniformly poor performance
across seeds (greedy 9.65–13.95, 7,100–8,500 steps). The divergence in step counts is informative: agents that learn to balance accumulate steps, while collapsed agents terminate
early.
The interaction between ϵ decay and random seed is central. For MLP seed 0,
the 4000-step decay provides ϵ > 0.5 for approximately 2000 steps (100–150 episodes),
during which the agent can discover reward-bearing trajectories. Seeds where exploration
fails to encounter balancing strategies before ϵ tightens are permanently trapped. This
dynamic is amplified for the VQC, where the quantum circuit’s gradient landscape may
make recovery from poor exploratory policies even harder.
The consistency of the collapse pattern — both agents across all DQN variants
(standard, Dueling, Double-Dueling) reach identically poor greedy evaluation scores at
100 episodes — supports the conclusion that neither the classical vs. quantum choice nor

the specific DQN variant matters at this budget. What matters is the interaction between
the ϵ schedule and the small model’s sensitivity.
Practical implications
The batched QNode result is the most actionable finding for practitioners. Reducing perupdate overhead from O(B) to O(1) QNode calls narrows the MLP–VQC wall-clock gap
from 44× to 5× on CPU simulation. For a B = 16 minibatch, this means 16 QNode calls
per update become 1 call — directly reducing the dominant cost of VQC-in-the-loop RL.
In environments with larger state spaces or deeper circuits, this gain scales linearly with
batch size.
The distillation results reveal a sharp interaction between representation and model
class that is independent of the RL training objective. Under raw features, the MLP can
learn the fuzzy policy (with sufficient supervised epochs) while the VQC cannot. Under
engineered fuzzy membership features, the VQC learns it perfectly while the MLP shows
high variance. This asymmetry — MLP wins on raw, VQC wins on fuzzy membership —
suggests that the two model classes exploit different aspects of the input representation,
which could guide future feature-engineering strategies for quantum RL.
The negative DQN results are valuable for calibrating expectations. A 22-parameter
VQC in a standard DQN pipeline, with standard hyperparameters and a modest epsilon
schedule, does not learn CartPole within 500 episodes. This is not a claim that VQCs
cannot learn CartPole at all — larger circuits, different hyperparameters, or alternative
training strategies may succeed. Rather, it is evidence that the out-of-the-box combination of a small VQC with standard DQN training is insufficient, and that practitioners
should expect to invest in hyperparameter tuning, exploration strategies, or representation
engineering to bridge the gap.

6. Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, the VQC was simulated classically; therefore,
wall-clock time does not represent execution on quantum hardware. Second, the experiment uses only CartPole-v1 and a small number of seeds (3) due to the submission timeline; higher seed counts (5–10) would provide more robust statistics, particularly given
the high seed variance observed in extended training runs. Third, the parameter budget
is extremely small, which makes the comparison controlled but also limits the representational capacity of both agents. In the main experiment, both learned agents perform
below a random policy, so the results characterize a failure regime rather than a successful
policy-learning regime. Fourth, only a shallow VQC (L1) is used in the main experiment.
Deeper circuits may be more expressive, but they also increase simulation cost and may
suffer from barren plateaus [McClean et al. 2018] in gradient-based optimization. Fifth,
the timing comparison between MLP (GPU) and VQC (CPU) conflates hardware and
simulation overheads; the reported VQC time multipliers should be interpreted as upper
bounds, not as precise quantum-vs-classical scaling.
The results should therefore be interpreted as a small controlled empirical study,
not as evidence for or against quantum advantage in reinforcement learning.
The fuzzy-teacher distillation experiment has a different status from the DQN experiments. It demonstrates that small models, including a VQC, can imitate a useful

fuzzy controller when aided by domain-informed fuzzy membership features, but under
raw features the VQC fails even in supervised optimization. Therefore, the distillation
results measure representation quality under structured features, not DQN learning from
reward. The fuzzy membership representation avoids direct action-recommendation encoding, but it is still a domain-informed representation and should be reported as such.

7. Conclusion
This paper implemented and evaluated a DQN-VQC agent against a parameter-matched
MLP-DQN baseline on CartPole-v1. We summarize the main findings against the four
research questions posed in Section 1.
RQ1 (Parameter-matched comparison). Under a 22–23 parameter budget and
100 training episodes, DQN-MLP and DQN-VQC are statistically indistinguishable (both
≈ 9.4 greedy evaluation, below the random baseline of 20.65). Neither learned the task.
Extending training to 500 episodes with a scaled ϵ schedule recovered learning in 1 of 3
MLP seeds (greedy 69.45) while VQC remained below random in all seeds.
RQ2 (DQN variants). Double DQN and Dueling DQN did not improve outcomes
at 100 episodes. All variants collapsed to the same single-action deterministic policy. The
Dueling architecture increased parameter counts marginally (23 → 27 for MLP, 22 → 27
for VQC) without measurable benefit. This confirms that the training budget, not the
value-estimation technique, dominates results at this scale.
RQ3 (Simulation cost). Batched QNode execution via PennyLane parameter
broadcasting reduced the VQC simulation overhead from 44× to 5× relative to MLP.
While VQC simulation remains more expensive than the classical equivalent, this optimization demonstrates that per-update circuit evaluation overhead can be substantially
reduced through batching. The 5× multiplier should be interpreted as an upper bound on
a CPU simulator; it conflates GPU-vs-CPU and PyTorch-vs-simulator overheads.
RQ4 (Representational capacity vs. reward extraction). The supervised distillation experiments reveal that representational adequacy depends on the input representation and model class. Under raw features, the MLP can learn the fuzzy policy (500.00
with extended training) while the VQC cannot (96.53). Under engineered fuzzy membership features, the VQC perfectly imitates the teacher at both L1 (22 params) and L3 (46
params) depths, while the MLP shows high variance (440.72 ± 102.68). The VQC can
therefore represent a useful control policy when the input features are well-structured and
the optimization signal is supervised. The DQN failure on raw features thus reflects both a
VQC representational limitation under raw inputs and a general reward-signal extraction
difficulty at this training budget.
The results carry several messages for future work. First, practitioners building
VQC-DQN pipelines should implement batched QNode evaluation, as it provides a significant and implementationally straightforward speedup. Second, the exploration schedule deserves careful tuning: the ϵ-decay-vs-steps ratio is critical for small-capacity agents
and should be treated as a primary hyperparameter, not an afterthought. Third, representation engineering (including domain-informed feature maps) appears to matter more
than model architecture or DQN variant choice at this scale. Fourth, future studies should
increase seeds to 5–10 and extend to additional environments (e.g., Acrobot, MountainCar) to test the generality of these findings. Finally, hybrid training strategies combining

reward-based DQN with fuzzy-guided exploration or curriculum learning remain an open
direction for bridging the gap between representational capacity and effective learning.
